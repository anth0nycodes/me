Design Engineering is a newer role you've probably heard about recently. In short, a design engineer sits at the intersection of design and engineering — which I personally consider a step up from frontend engineering.

## Frontend Engineering vs Design Engineering

It's easy to confuse the two, especially when so much of the work overlaps. But in my opinion, the main thing that separates a frontend engineer from a design engineer is one thing: taste.

So what is taste? Taste is the intuition you develop over time from consuming what's considered "good design." Have you ever looked at an animation or a piece of UI and just felt "yeah, this is it" — or the opposite, "I don't like how that feels"? That gut reaction is the essence of taste.

Digging deeper: a frontend engineer's sole goal is to translate designs into performant, optimized visual code. Design engineering is a level beyond that. A design engineer does everything a frontend engineer does, but with a higher degree of product ownership. As a design engineer, you might be the person handling both the design and the frontend — or at the very least, someone who can resonate with product designers on a deeper level.

## How to approach design from scratch

A good starting point is to truly understand the context: what company, what industry, who the target audience is. Having all of this prerequisite knowledge up front simplifies designing general pages like landing pages or other non-core-product UI.

For product UI, take those same criteria into account — but focus more on: what exactly are you trying to accomplish? Say you're building a multi-step form. You really need a solid understanding of:

1. What are you trying to accomplish?
2. How should it feel?
3. What's the bare-minimum, [lo-fi](https://www.uxtweak.com/ux-glossary/low-fidelity-prototype-lo-fi-prototype/) layout that can sufficiently achieve each step?

From there, you can apply your foundations of design — color theory, visual hierarchy, and so on — then refine your prototypes and even A/B test different variations of the same thing.

## Where I think design is headed

There are tons of AI tools being progressively adopted for design. If you're just starting out (like me), the best way to get better is to simply consume and reimplement. Gather a list of websites or products whose UI you really like, then try to reimplement snippets of them — a specific component, a section, whatever. This trains you to visualize the hierarchy of a component, and to consider the right palettes, typography, and spacing. Early on, the best way to learn is just to practice.

Once you're more comfortable, you can bring all of that prior design knowledge over to AI design tools. One that I know of is [wonder.so](https://wonder.so) (thanks for showing me this [Arman!](https://x.com/ksw_arman)).

## Closing

Hopefully by this point, you should have a sense of what is and isn't good design — so you can then iterate with an AI agent and use your fundamental knowledge (the vocab, the principles) to reach what you're envisioning much faster. And even when you don't yet know what you're envisioning, you can prototype and talk it through with the agent to get there.
